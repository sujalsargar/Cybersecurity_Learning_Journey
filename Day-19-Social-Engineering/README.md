Day 19 - Social Engineering
Objective

Understand what social engineering is, learn how attackers exploit human psychology instead of only technical vulnerabilities, explore the six universal psychological triggers, study common social engineering attacks, and understand the lessons from the 2023 MGM Resorts cyberattack.

Lesson of the Day

Today, I learned about Social Engineering.

One of the most important lessons was:

Social engineering is not a single technique. It is a category of attacks that targets humans rather than only systems.

Modern security can succeed technically and still fail socially.

An organization may have:

Strong firewalls
Updated systems
Encryption
Endpoint protection
Security monitoring

But an attacker may still succeed by convincing a person to:

Reveal information
Share credentials
Approve access
Reset a password
Open a malicious file
Trust a fake identity

This is why social engineering remains one of the most important areas of cybersecurity.

What is Social Engineering?

Social engineering is the manipulation of people into performing actions or revealing information that helps an attacker.

Instead of directly attacking technology, the attacker may target:

Employees
Customers
Help desk staff
Executives
Contractors
Administrators
Business partners

The attacker attempts to exploit normal human behavior.

The Human Attack Surface

Today's lesson taught me that humans can become part of the attack surface.

Attackers may exploit:

Trust
Fear
Curiosity
Respect for authority
Desire to help
Time pressure
Excitement
Familiarity

The attacker does not always need a sophisticated technical exploit.

Sometimes, the attack begins with a convincing story.

Six Universal Triggers of Social Engineering

I learned six important psychological triggers commonly used in social engineering.

1. Authority

People are more likely to follow instructions from someone they believe has authority.

An attacker may pretend to be:

A manager
A CEO
An IT administrator
A bank employee
A government official
A security team member

Example:

"This is the IT security team.
We detected suspicious activity.
Send us your verification code immediately."

The attacker uses perceived authority to pressure the victim.

2. Urgency

Urgency creates pressure and reduces careful thinking.

Examples:

"Your account will be suspended in 10 minutes."
"Immediate action required."
"Your payment has failed. Verify now."

The goal is to make the victim react before verifying the request.

3. Scarcity

People may act quickly when they believe something is limited.

Examples:

Limited-time offer
Only two positions remaining
Last chance to apply
Offer expires today
Limited internship opportunity

Attackers use scarcity to reduce careful decision-making.

4. Reciprocity

People often feel pressure to return a favor.

An attacker may:

Offer free help
Give something valuable
Provide a small benefit
Pretend to solve a problem

Then request something in return.

The request may involve:

Information
Access
Credentials
Installing software
5. Social Proof

People are more likely to trust something when they believe others already trust it.

Examples:

"Everyone in your department has already completed this verification."
"Your colleagues have already signed up."

Attackers use the behavior of others to make a request appear legitimate.

6. Liking

People are more likely to trust someone they like or relate to.

Attackers may create familiarity through:

Shared interests
Friendly communication
Similar backgrounds
Compliments
Common connections

The attacker builds trust before making a request.

Common Social Engineering Attacks

Today, I also learned about several types of social engineering attacks.

1. Phishing

Phishing uses fraudulent messages to trick victims into:

Clicking malicious links
Revealing credentials
Downloading malicious files
Sharing sensitive information

Phishing attacks are usually sent to many people.

2. Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a targeted form of phishing.

The attacker researches a specific:

Person
Employee
Department
Organization

The message is personalized to appear more convincing.

3. Whaling

Whaling targets senior or high-value individuals.

Examples:

CEOs
CFOs
Directors
Senior managers
Executives

These individuals may have access to sensitive information or financial systems.

4. Vishing

Vishing means:

Voice Phishing

The attacker uses phone calls to manipulate the victim.

Examples:

Fake bank calls
Fake technical support
Fake government officials
Fake security alerts
5. Smishing

Smishing means:

SMS Phishing

Attackers send fraudulent text messages.

Examples:

"Your bank account has been blocked.
Verify immediately."
"Your package delivery failed.
Update your address here."
6. Baiting

Baiting uses curiosity or something attractive to trick the victim.

Examples:

Free software
Free movie download
Free USB drive
Prize
Exclusive document

The victim interacts with the bait and may expose the system to risk.

7. Pretexting

Pretexting involves creating a believable false story or identity.

The attacker may pretend to be:

An employee
A manager
IT support
A vendor
A customer
A bank representative

The attacker creates a scenario to convince the victim to provide information or access.

8. Tailgating

Tailgating is a physical social engineering attack.

An attacker follows an authorized person into a restricted area.

Example:

Employee scans access card
        ↓
Door opens
        ↓
Attacker follows behind
        ↓
Restricted area accessed

The attacker may rely on politeness to avoid being challenged.

9. Business Email Compromise (BEC)

Business Email Compromise is a targeted attack where criminals impersonate trusted business identities.

Examples:

CEO fraud
Fake invoice requests
Payment redirection
Vendor impersonation
Payroll changes

A typical example:

"Hi,

I am in an urgent meeting.

Please transfer the payment immediately
and do not call me right now."

The attacker combines:

Authority
Urgency
Trust
How Attackers Prepare Social Engineering Attacks

One important lesson was that attackers often collect publicly available information before contacting a target.

Sources may include:

Company websites
Professional profiles
Public social media
Job postings
Press releases
Public documents
Employee information

The attacker may use this information to create a believable pretext.

Example of a Pretext

An attacker may learn:

Employee name
Job title
Manager name
Company department
Technology used by the company

Then create a message such as:

"Hi,

I'm calling from the IT team.

Your manager requested an urgent account update.

I just need you to confirm your employee details."

Because the attacker knows real information, the request may appear legitimate.

Case Study - MGM Resorts Cyberattack (2023)

Today, I also learned about the 2023 MGM Resorts cyberattack.

The incident became an important example of how social engineering can contribute to a major cybersecurity incident.

Public reporting indicated that attackers used information about employees and contacted the organization's IT help desk.

The attackers reportedly impersonated an employee and manipulated the support process to gain access.

Simplified Attack Flow
Public Information Gathering
        ↓
Employee Research
        ↓
Create a Convincing Pretext
        ↓
Contact Help Desk
        ↓
Impersonate Employee
        ↓
Manipulate Support Process
        ↓
Gain Unauthorized Access
        ↓
Further Compromise
Important Lesson from the MGM Case

The attackers did not necessarily begin by breaking encryption or directly exploiting a complex technical vulnerability.

Instead, the attack demonstrated how weaknesses in:

Identity verification
Help desk procedures
Access controls
Human trust

can contribute to serious security incidents.

This reinforced one of today's biggest lessons:

A technically secure system can still be compromised through human manipulation.

How to Defend Against Social Engineering
1. Verify Identity

Never trust a request only because someone knows personal or company information.

Use an independent trusted method to verify identity.

2. Slow Down

Urgency is a common manipulation technique.

Before acting:

Pause
Think
Verify
3. Limit Public Information

Be careful about publicly sharing:

Personal details
Internal company information
Employee structures
Travel plans
Sensitive work information

Public information can help attackers build convincing pretexts.

4. Use MFA

Multi-Factor Authentication adds another layer of protection.

However, users must also be aware of:

MFA fatigue
Fake approval requests
Social engineering attempts targeting authentication processes
5. Verify Financial Requests

For payment changes or sensitive transactions:

Confirm through another trusted channel
Call a known number
Follow approval procedures
6. Improve Help Desk Verification

Support teams should use strong identity verification before:

Resetting passwords
Changing MFA
Modifying accounts
Granting access
What I Learned
Social engineering targets people rather than only technology.
Human psychology can become part of the attack surface.
Attackers use authority, urgency, scarcity, reciprocity, social proof, and liking.
Publicly available information can help attackers create believable pretexts.
Phishing is only one type of social engineering.
Spear phishing targets specific individuals.
Whaling targets executives.
Vishing uses phone calls.
Smishing uses SMS messages.
Tailgating targets physical access.
BEC targets business communication and financial processes.
Strong technical security can still fail when identity verification and human processes are weak.
Key Takeaways

✅ Social engineering is a category of attacks targeting human behavior.

✅ The six universal triggers are:

Authority
Urgency
Scarcity
Reciprocity
Social Proof
Liking

✅ Attackers research targets before creating convincing stories.

✅ Limiting unnecessary public information reduces attacker intelligence.

✅ Always verify unusual or urgent requests through another trusted channel.

✅ Strong security requires both technical controls and secure human processes.

Reflection

Today's lesson changed how I think about cybersecurity attacks.

Earlier, I often imagined attackers mainly using malware, exploits, or technical vulnerabilities. Now I understand that attackers can sometimes achieve their goals by manipulating people.

The 2023 MGM Resorts case was especially interesting because it showed how publicly available information, a convincing pretext, and weaknesses in identity verification can contribute to a major incident.

The biggest lesson for me was:

Modern security can succeed technically and still fail socially.

This is why cybersecurity is not only about securing computers. It is also about securing people, processes, trust, and decision-making.
