Day 67 - Threats, Vulnerabilities and Risks
Objective

Understand the difference between threats, vulnerabilities, and risks, learn how they are connected, and understand how cybersecurity professionals assess and respond to risks.

Lesson of the Day

Today, I learned about Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risks.

These three concepts are closely connected.

A simple way to remember them is:

Threat + Vulnerability = Risk

A threat creates the possibility of harm, while a vulnerability provides a weakness that the threat can exploit.

What is a Threat?

A threat is anything that has the potential to cause harm to a system.

It is a potential source of danger.

Examples can include:

Attackers
Malware
Phishing attempts
Insider threats
Natural events
Other harmful activities

A threat by itself does not necessarily mean that damage will occur.

What is a Vulnerability?

A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat can potentially exploit.

A vulnerability can exist in:

Software
Hardware
System configuration
Processes
Security controls
People

Examples include:

Weak passwords
Outdated software
Misconfigured systems
Excessive permissions
Unnecessary open ports
Threat vs Vulnerability

The difference is simple:

Threat

Something that could cause harm.

Vulnerability

A weakness that could be exploited.

For example:

Weak Password
     ↓
Vulnerability

Attacker
     ↓
Threat

If the attacker exploits the weak password, the risk becomes real.

Threat + Vulnerability = Risk

The relationship I learned today is:

Threat
   +
Vulnerability
   ↓
Risk

A threat without a relevant vulnerability may not be able to cause damage.

Similarly, a vulnerability without a threat exploiting it may not cause immediate harm.

The important point is:

Damage occurs when a threat can exploit a vulnerability.

Components of Risk

Risk can be considered using two important components:

1. Likelihood

How likely is the event to happen?

For example:

Is the system exposed to the internet?
Is the vulnerability easy to exploit?
Are attackers likely to target the system?
2. Impact

How serious would the consequences be if it happened?

For example:

Would sensitive information be exposed?
Would a service become unavailable?
Would important data be modified?
Would the organization suffer financial or operational damage?

A simple way to think about it is:

Risk
 ↓
Likelihood + Impact

Both should be considered when prioritizing security risks.

Four Ways to Handle Risk

Today's lesson introduced four common ways to respond to risks.

1. Reduce

Reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk by applying security controls.

Examples:

Patch vulnerable software.
Improve authentication.
Apply least privilege.
Use firewalls.
Enable monitoring.
2. Accept

Sometimes an organization may decide that a particular risk is acceptable.

This does not mean the risk does not exist.

It means the organization understands the risk and intentionally decides to accept it.

3. Transfer

Transfer some or all of the financial or operational impact of a risk to another party.

For example, organizations may use insurance or contractual arrangements as part of risk management.

4. Avoid

Avoid the activity or situation that creates the risk.

For example:

If a service is not necessary, removing it may eliminate the associated exposure.

Security Risk Analysis Framework

The lesson introduced a simple framework for analyzing security risks.

Step 1: Identify Risks

Determine what could go wrong.

Step 2: Identify Vulnerabilities

Find the weaknesses that could allow the threat to succeed.

Step 3: Assess Risk

Consider:

Likelihood
Impact
Step 4: Choose a Response

Decide whether to:

Reduce
Accept
Transfer
Avoid

The overall process looks like:

Identify Risk
     ↓
Identify Vulnerability
     ↓
Assess Likelihood & Impact
     ↓
Choose Response
     ↓
Apply / Monitor Controls
Connecting Risk to the CIA Triad

Today's lesson also connected directly with Day 66 - CIA Triad.

Threats can affect:

Confidentiality

Unauthorized people gain access to information.

Integrity

Information is changed or corrupted without authorization.

Availability

Systems or information become unavailable.

So when analyzing a threat, an analyst should ask:

Which CIA property could be affected?

For example:

Threat
  ↓
Vulnerability
  ↓
Risk
  ↓
CIA Impact
  ↓
Security Response

This connects risk management with the CIA Triad.

Example

Consider a system with an outdated application.

Threat

An attacker may attempt to exploit the application.

Vulnerability

The application contains an unpatched security weakness.

Risk

The attacker may exploit the weakness and compromise the system.

CIA Impact

Depending on the attack, it could affect:

Confidentiality
Integrity
Availability
Response

A reasonable response could be to reduce the risk by applying the appropriate security update and adding monitoring or other controls.

What I Learned
A threat is a potential source of harm.
A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat can exploit.
Threat + vulnerability creates risk.
Risk involves likelihood and impact.
A vulnerability does not automatically mean damage will occur.
Risks can be reduced, accepted, transferred, or avoided.
Risk analysis involves identifying risks, identifying vulnerabilities, assessing risk, and choosing a response.
Threats and risks can affect confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
The CIA Triad helps understand the potential impact of a risk.
Key Takeaways

✅ Threat = Potential danger

✅ Vulnerability = Weakness

✅ Risk = Threat exploiting a vulnerability

✅ Consider both likelihood and impact.

✅ Risk responses include Reduce, Accept, Transfer, and Avoid.

✅ Always consider how a risk affects Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.

Reflection

Today's lesson helped me understand the difference between three terms that are often used together in cybersecurity: threat, vulnerability, and risk.

The biggest thing I learned is that a vulnerability alone does not necessarily mean that damage will happen. There must be a threat capable of exploiting that weakness for the risk to become significant.

I also learned that cybersecurity is not about eliminating every possible risk. Instead, professionals need to understand the likelihood and impact of risks and then choose an appropriate response.

Connecting this lesson with the CIA Triad made the concept even clearer because it helps identify what could actually be affected by a threat.

My biggest takeaway is:

Don't just ask "What is the vulnerability?" Ask who could exploit it, how likely it is, what the impact would be, and how the risk should be handled.

Quote of the Day

"A vulnerability is a weakness. Risk begins when a threat can exploit it."
