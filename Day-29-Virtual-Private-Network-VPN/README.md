Day 29 - Virtual Private Network (VPN)
Objective

Understand what a Virtual Private Network (VPN) is, how it works, what it actually protects, its limitations, and when it should be used.

Lesson of the Day

Today, I learned about Virtual Private Networks (VPNs).

A VPN creates an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server, helping protect your internet traffic while it travels across the network.

One of the most important concepts I learned is:

A VPN moves the trust boundary.

Without a VPN:

Your Internet Service Provider (ISP) and local network can see much of your internet activity (although HTTPS encrypts website content).

With a VPN:

Your encrypted traffic passes through the VPN provider first, so you are placing your trust in the VPN provider instead of your ISP or local network.
What is a VPN?

A Virtual Private Network (VPN) is a service that creates a secure, encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN server.

All internet traffic travels through this encrypted tunnel before reaching its final destination.

This helps protect your traffic while it is in transit.

Four Things a VPN Actually Does
1. Encrypts Your Internet Traffic

A VPN encrypts your data before it travels over the internet.

This makes it much more difficult for others on the same network to read your traffic while it is in transit.

2. Hides Browsing Activity from Your ISP

Because your traffic is encrypted inside the VPN tunnel, your ISP cannot easily see the websites you are accessing in the same way it could without a VPN.

Instead, it mainly sees that you are connected to a VPN server.

3. Changes Your Online Location

Websites usually see the VPN server's public IP address rather than your own.

This can make it appear as though you are browsing from another city or country.

4. Allows Secure Remote Access

Organizations commonly use VPNs to allow employees to securely connect to internal company networks while working remotely.

What a VPN Does NOT Do

One of today's biggest lessons was understanding the limitations of VPNs.

A VPN is not a complete cybersecurity solution.

It does not:

1. Make You Anonymous

A VPN improves privacy, but it does not make you completely anonymous online.

2. Protect You from Malware

A VPN cannot prevent malware infections, viruses, or ransomware.

Endpoint security and safe browsing practices are still required.

3. Hide Your Account Identity

If you log in to services such as Google, Facebook, or other online accounts, those services still know who you are.

A VPN does not hide your identity from accounts you authenticate to.

4. Add Much Security to HTTPS Websites

Most modern websites already use HTTPS, which encrypts communication between your browser and the website.

A VPN mainly protects the connection between your device and the VPN server.

5. Stop Website Tracking

Websites can still track users through:

Cookies
Logged-in accounts
Browser fingerprinting
Other tracking technologies

A VPN alone does not prevent these methods.

When You Should Use a VPN

A VPN is especially useful in situations such as:

Public or Untrusted Wi-Fi

Using a VPN on public Wi-Fi helps protect your traffic from others on the same network.

Remote Work

Many organizations require employees to use a VPN when connecting to internal systems from outside the office.

Accessing Region-Specific Content

A VPN can allow access to services or content available in another country by routing traffic through a server in that location.

Bypassing Network Restrictions

In some situations, a VPN may help users access the internet when their ISP or local network applies restrictions.

When You Should NOT Rely on a VPN

A VPN should not replace good security practices.

Important reminders include:

Do not use a VPN instead of practicing good cybersecurity hygiene.
Be cautious with free VPN services, as some may collect or sell user data.
Do not believe every marketing claim made by VPN providers.

A VPN is only one part of a layered security approach.

What I Learned
A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between a device and a VPN server.
Using a VPN changes the trust boundary from the ISP to the VPN provider.
VPNs help protect data while it is in transit.
VPNs can change the visible public IP address.
VPNs are useful for remote work and public Wi-Fi.
VPNs do not provide complete anonymity or protect against malware.
Good cybersecurity requires multiple layers of protection beyond a VPN.
Key Takeaways

✅ A VPN encrypts internet traffic.

✅ A VPN helps protect traffic on public networks.

✅ A VPN changes your visible public IP address.

✅ VPNs are useful for secure remote access.

✅ A VPN does not make you anonymous.

✅ A VPN does not replace antivirus software, HTTPS, or safe online behavior.

Reflection

Today's lesson helped me understand what a VPN actually does—and, just as importantly, what it does not do.

Before this lesson, I thought of a VPN mainly as a privacy tool. Now I understand that its primary role is to create an encrypted tunnel and move the trust boundary from my ISP or local network to the VPN provider.

I also learned that while VPNs improve privacy and security in many situations, they are not a substitute for good cybersecurity practices such as using HTTPS, keeping systems updated, and protecting devices from malware.

My biggest takeaway is:

A VPN is an important security tool, but it works best as one layer in a broader cybersecurity strategy.

Quote of the Day

"A VPN protects your connection—not your every action."
